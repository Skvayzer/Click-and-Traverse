# Time-priced risk teaches the gap sprint

No published work reports this exact failure, a learned policy that cannot cross a gap slowly but gets through with momentum, yet each half of it is documented, and together they point at what training charges per second rather than at what the robot perceives. Classical potential-field studies name "no passage between closely spaced obstacles" an inherent flaw, a force balance at the doorway with no speed term ([Koren & Borenstein 1991](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/integrated1/borenstein_potential_field_limitations.pdf)), and robots that perceive obstacles only as sampled points reach the goal about half as often as robots given analytic shapes, because they stop in local minima ([Huber et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.04928)); that supports your hypothesis that 13 body samples can wall off a gap the body must rotate through. But a wall produces stalls, not sprints, and in this stack the operator speed is applied after the field projection, so the command itself contains no slow-speed imbalance: the speed dependence is learned. Learned-locomotion papers document exactly that kind of learned haste wherever time near danger is priced, with robots sprinting through obstacles to cut per-second penalties ([Zhuang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05665)), waiting and then sprinting ([Rudin et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.12827)), and aligning slowly before bursting across beams ([Zhang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.10484)), while engineered navigators do the opposite and slow at doors, the RHINO robot to about 20 cm/s ([Fox et al. 1997](https://publications.ri.cmu.edu/storage/publications/pub_files/pub1/fox_dieter_1997_1/fox_dieter_1997_1.pdf)). Your V3–V6 fine-tunes already apply the published reward fixes and cut gap speed from about 0.7 to 0.33–0.43 m/s; what still prices time inside the gap is hard contact termination, velocity pushes of 0.1–1.0 m/s every 5–10 s, and a one-second discount horizon, none of which either runtime test could reach. The plan the literature implies is to run five cheap diagnostic tests, then remove the per-second hazards from tight zones and charge approach rate instead of presence, schedule the sideways turn and the slow speed before the gap, and confirm a slow sidestep gait exists, leaving denser perception for last.

## Classical fields stall at doorways, and none needed momentum

The closest prior observation is Koren and Borenstein's 1991 study of potential-field navigation on CARMEL, a 125 kg robot running at up to 0.8 m/s. They list four problems they call inherent to the method: traps in local minima, **no passage between closely spaced obstacles**, oscillation near obstacles, and **oscillation in narrow passages**. Their account of the second is a pure force balance: at a door frame, "the sum of all repulsive forces … points straight away from the opening between the two obstacles. Depending on the relative magnitude of the target-directed force Ft, the robot will either approach the opening further, or it will turn away" ([Koren & Borenstein 1991](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/integrated1/borenstein_potential_field_limitations.pdf)). In the same group's field runs the robot handled obstacles placed at least 1.8 m apart, more than twice its 0.8 m diameter, but "sometimes … would not pass through a doorway, because the repulsive forces from both sides of the doorway resulted in a force that pushed the robot away" ([Borenstein & Koren 1991](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/integrated1/borenstein_VFHisto.pdf)). Nothing in that mechanism depends on speed. Their oscillation analysis does: the corridor stability condition L^(n+1) > 2nWⁿV(T+τ)f tightens with speed V, steering lag τ and sampling time T, so at 0.8 m/s a corridor with 0.8 m to each wall went unstable while one with 1.2 m stayed stable, and the authors remarked that groups working "at slow speeds" conceal these disadvantages ([Koren & Borenstein 1991](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/integrated1/borenstein_potential_field_limitations.pdf)).

Every engineered navigator since then treats speed as the enemy of narrow passage. The dynamic window approach admits only velocities that can stop before the nearest obstacle and widens its safety margin linearly with speed, so RHINO "decelerates to approximately 20 cm per second in the vicinity of the door" on 80–110 cm doorways, and at 75 cm/s it could not make a turn through an open door that it managed at 40 cm/s ([Fox, Burgard & Thrun 1997](https://publications.ri.cmu.edu/storage/publications/pub_files/pub1/fox_dieter_1997_1/fox_dieter_1997_1.pdf)). VFH lowers travel speed whenever the global plan calls for a doorway ([Borenstein & Koren 1991](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/integrated1/borenstein_VFHisto.pdf)), fast-marching-square navigation assigns speeds that fall to zero at obstacles ([Gómez, FM² thesis and library](https://github.com/jvgomez/fastmarching)), and the speed-and-separation rule now in ISO 10218-2 sizes the protective distance as reaction distance plus braking distance plus a static tolerance ([Bajrami et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.07621)). With illustrative values of 0.15 s reaction time and 1 m/s² braking, that structure asks for about 2 cm of speed-dependent margin at 0.1 m/s and about 54 cm at 0.9 m/s (our arithmetic). In this tradition **slowness is what licenses small clearance**, and a 0.9 m/s pass through a 0.44 m gap is precisely the case the rules forbid. Human crowds follow the same logic: measured pedestrian interactions collapse onto one curve when plotted against time-to-collision rather than distance ([Karamouzas, Skinner & Guy 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.1082)).

The classical cure for the doorway stall is to make repulsion depend on approach rate. Krogh's generalized potential is the inverse of the reserve time left to stop before an obstacle, and Krogh and Thorpe state that potentials "which are both position and velocity dependent" eliminate "the possibility of the stalling at local minima in the potential field" ([Krogh & Thorpe 1986](https://publications.ri.cmu.edu/storage/publications/pub_files/pub3/krogh_bruce_1986_1/krogh_bruce_1986_1.pdf)). The Faverjon–Tournassoud velocity damper bounds only the approach rate, ḋ ≥ −ξ(d − d_s)/(d_i − d_s), and leaves tangential motion free ([Kanehiro et al. 2008](http://www.roboticsproceedings.org/rss04/p20.pdf)), and a barrier filter built from an APF's own repulsive term, which intervenes "only when necessary rather than always", took a quadrotor through a 0.7 m doorway on hardware where the APF failed ([Singletary et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.09819)). The same physics reads the real-robot symptom. A position-only barrier acts at full strength on a robot at rest, so a robot moving quasi-statically stops where the barrier balances the pull, while a robot carrying kinetic energy can coast over a rise up to that energy, the flip side of Rimon and Koditschek's result that potential-field safety needs a speed limit that depends on position ([Rimon & Koditschek 1992](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/r/rimon_koditschek_potential.pdf)). **"Repelled when slow, through with momentum" is the fingerprint of a position-dependent barrier on the approach path**, not of an approach-rate limit, which would leave a slow robot alone (our inference; no source measured this on a legged robot).

|---|---|---|---|
| No passage at a doorway ([Koren & Borenstein 1991](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/integrated1/borenstein_potential_field_limitations.pdf)) | Summed repulsion points out of the opening | None; position only | Matches "repelled" at the gap mouth |
| Oscillation in corridors ([Koren & Borenstein 1991](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/integrated1/borenstein_potential_field_limitations.pdf)) | Opposing walls plus control lag | Worse as V(τ+T) grows | Predicts slow is safer, the opposite of the G1 |
| Whole-body local minimum ([Barraquand et al. 1992](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/b/barraquand_langlois_latombe_potential.pdf)) | Control points compete | None | Predicts a stall, not a burst |
| Coasting over a barrier (our inference) | Position-only barrier plus inertia | Needs enough kinetic energy | Matches "passes only with momentum" |

## Thirteen samples can wall off a gap but cannot explain a sprint

The literature supports the first half of your hypothesis. Barraquand, Langlois and Latombe showed that per-point potentials that are each free of local minima do not combine into a whole-body field free of them: the potentials "are computed for a finite (usually small) number of control points", those points "are competing among themselves", and plain summation makes conflicts "frequent, producing numerous local minima" ([Barraquand, Langlois & Latombe 1992](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/b/barraquand_langlois_latombe_potential.pdf)). Huber, Billard and Slotine measured the cost of sampling directly: with only sampled obstacle points, **45% of 100 trajectories reached the goal, against 83%** with analytic obstacle shapes, because "trajectories often end in a local minimum when using sampled data of obstacles only" ([Huber, Billard & Slotine 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.04928)). The CAT paper names the humanoid version itself, warning that left- and right-side fields "cancel each other out, leading to a multi-modal dilemma where the robot becomes trapped in a local minimum or exhibits oscillatory behavior". Its fix is a priority weighting whose urgency factor, λ·max(−∇d·v_k, 0.5)·exp(−d), grows with approach velocity but is floored at 0.5, so a slow approach is still repelled ([Xue et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.16035)). Fixed sample points add a switching problem as the body rotates: when the active closest point jumps between features, Kanehiro et al.'s constrained body oscillates and eventually collides ([Kanehiro et al. 2008](http://www.roboticsproceedings.org/rss04/p20.pdf)).

Reading the code shows where such a barrier can live in this stack and where it cannot. The policy sees 13 sites, 11 field sites plus two elbow probes, each with a unit guidance direction, a boundary normal and a signed distance clipped at 0.5 m, plus hand and elbow distance rates (`outputs/deploy/slow_V6_u050/deploy_spec.json`); it gets no gap width, nothing beyond half a metre, and no goal distance. The position-only push-back comes from the reward: clearance and signed-distance costs, termination on hand, elbow and body contact, and, in room-style and joystick worlds, a tracking target from `safe_velocity_target` (`cat_mjlab/task_math.py`) that removes the command's inward component at seven points (pelvis, hands, knees, shoulders) at full weight inside 0.25 m. At the mouth of a narrow passage, any pose that leaves a hand or shoulder facing a flank wall therefore earns a **stop target at every commanded speed**, and only the sideways pose earns the full command, which is Koren and Borenstein's doorway balance in multi-point form written into the reward (our inference from the code). The explicit command carries no such balance. `_operator_command` (`cat_mjlab/task.py`) computes the projection at the nominal 0.7 scale, keeps only its direction, applies the operator speed afterwards, and stops only if the nominal projection drops below 0.05 m/s; in a symmetric approach the two hands' corrections remove about two-fifths of the forward component, and normalization restores the full commanded speed. "Repulsion beats attraction at slow speed" would hold for an additive controller whose pull shrinks with the commanded speed against a fixed push, and that structure appears nowhere in this pipeline. If the policy behaves that way, it learned to.

The evidence that richer perception would make the robot slower is thin. CAT's own ablations favor its fields: on the real robot, the same specialists distilled onto HumanoidPF, voxel grids and elevation maps passed side gaps 5/5, 2/5 and 1/5 ([Xue et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.16035)). The one learned system that explicitly "slows down in the tight spaces", ABS, perceives through 11 sparse rays, the same input as the agile policy that rushes, and gets its slowing from a velocity-conditioned reach-avoid value plus a separate recovery tracker ([He et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.17583)). Voxel- and map-based imitation systems do beat the released CAT generalist on sideways and contact-free passage, 97.7% against 49.8% on MTC's sidewalk scenes and 70.3% against 14.0% contact-free success in PASSAGE's comparison, but each comparison changes training data, reference motion and controller scope at once ([Wang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21107); [Ma et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18732)). The CAT paper reports no commanded or achieved speeds at all, and its release trains what is effectively one field-derived speed with a stop below 0.2 m/s ([CAT code](https://github.com/GalaxyGeneralRobotics/Click-and-Traverse)), a gap your log-uniform 0.1–0.6 m/s operator speeds already close. No study varies perception alone while measuring speed compliance. Sparse sampling plausibly builds the barrier at the mouth and denies the policy the look-ahead it needs to commit early; it does not explain why the policy accelerates.

## Learned policies sprint wherever time near danger is priced

Learned-locomotion papers describe rushing as an artifact of the objective, never of perception. Robot Parkour Learning multiplies its penetration penalty by forward velocity "to prevent the robot from exploiting the penetration reward by sprinting through the obstacles to avoid high cumulative penalties over time" ([Zhuang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05665)). Rudin et al. found that under discounting with per-step penalties "the policy learns to wait until the last moment when it suddenly runs fast towards the target", and that a reward window that is too short teaches it to "jump or lean towards the target at the last moment" ([Rudin et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.12827)). On balance beams, Zhang et al.'s ANYmal learned the closest published match to "turns sideways, then bursts through", "first slowly aligning itself towards the center and then making a speed burst to quickly traverse the terrain", and the authors responded: "We slightly extend the episode length to slow down the aggressive motions" ([Zhang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.10484)). Pardo et al.'s time-aware agents take riskier actions as a deadline nears, and their Hopper ends in a "photo finish" ([Pardo et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00378)). Termination belongs to the same family, because ending an episode is a transition discount of zero ([White 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.01995)), and CaT makes the link explicit by scaling future rewards by Π γ(1−δ) ([Chane-Sane et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.18765)). If each step inside a gap carries a contact-termination probability p that does not grow with speed, survival (1−p)ⁿ is maximized by minimizing n, the number of steps spent inside, and **rushing and refusing become two exits of one exposure-minimizing incentive**: rush when passage is feasible, refuse when it is not (our inference). Biology points the same way, since children, who discount reward more steeply than adults, make faster saccades ([Shadmehr et al. 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20685993/)).

V3–V6 already implement most of the published reward fixes, which is consistent with gap speed falling from about 0.7 to 0.33–0.43 m/s at a 0.15 m/s command. The table audits what still prices time inside the gap in the V6 training configuration (`cat_mjlab/config.py`; `outputs/deploy/slow_V6_u050/deploy_spec.json`).

| Pressure toward short exposure | Published mechanism | Status in V6 training | Remaining |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discounted goal bonus of 2000, 0.4–1.4 m past each obstacle | Time-to-goal value and last-moment lunges ([Rudin et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.12827)) | V5 pays it only for an arrival within the speed limit | Residual discounting |
| Per-second clearance and SDF costs | Sprinting through to cut cumulative penalties ([Zhuang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05665)) | V4 charges per metre at the larger of commanded and actual speed | Closed |
| Cosine field alignment | Progress capped at the command, min(⟨v, d̂⟩, v_cmd) ([Cheng et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14341)) | V3 pays along-field speed capped at the command | Closed |

Two of the open rows are per-second by construction, so V4's per-metre conversion cannot reach them. The pushes are base-velocity impulses in a random horizontal direction (`cat_mjlab/task.py`), and V6 trained with them on. They arrive every T seconds with T drawn uniformly between 5 and 10 s, so the chance that one lands while the robot spends t seconds in a tight zone is t·E[1/T] ≈ 0.14·t; for an assumed 0.4 m tight zone that is **about 37% at 0.15 m/s against 6% at 0.9 m/s** (our arithmetic). Whatever fraction of those pushes ends in a hand or elbow touch becomes a terminated episode, so a slow pass carries six times the push-induced termination risk of the burst. Whether that is large enough to explain the residual 2–3× overspeed is unknown, and logging terminations by cause answers it. The discount amplifies any such difference: at γ = 0.98 per 20 ms step, reaching a 2000 reward one second sooner is worth about 1270 when it is one second away, and at γ = 0.999 about 100, a 13-fold reduction that never reaches zero (our arithmetic). One smaller term deserves a check. The stall test compares a progress average with a one-second time constant against 0.2 of the command, so it charges the turn-in-place pause at the mouth, and a burst clears it fastest. Gaertner et al. hit the model-based version of this and clipped ANYmal's position-tracking error at 15 cm to prevent "a sudden pull towards the commanded trajectory once the robot negotiates the blocking obstacle" ([Gaertner et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.13987)).

The two runtime tests could not reach any of these terms, so their null results do not discriminate between hypotheses. Moving the goal 3 m further changes nothing the policy observes near the gap: guidance enters the observation as a unit direction (`cat_mjlab/task.py` normalizes it), the field generator holds guidance speed constant beyond 0.3 m from the goal (`procedural_obstacle_generation/pf_modular.py`), signed distances are clipped at 0.5 m, and there is no goal-distance or time-to-go input. The burst is a reaction to local gap geometry, learned in training where every success line sat 0.4–1.4 m past its obstacle. Gait frequency is a weak speed lever even for policies trained on it, since Walk These Ways reports that "enforcing low frequency (2Hz) makes high speeds less attainable", not impossible ([Margolis & Agrawal 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.03238)), and this policy trained only on 1.3–1.5 Hz clocks, so a different runtime frequency is simply out of distribution.

## Published fixes charge approach rate and turn the robot before the gap

Systems that are both slow and close charge approach rate, not presence. Molnar et al. turned an obstacle barrier into a closed-form safe velocity, q̇_s = q̇_d + max{−n_oᵀq̇_d − α(d − r), 0}·n_o, which a Unitree A1 tracked by forward- and side-stepping; only the excess approach velocity is removed, so a slow command is modified least ([Molnar et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.09047)). CBF-RL moved the same structure into RL training on the Unitree G1, with a closed-form filter on the planar velocity during training and a reward that is zero unless the filter activates, and the deployed policy, running without a filter, "modulates its own velocity to avoid the obstacle, even when the velocity command prompts it to do so" ([Yang et al. 2025, CBF-RL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.14959)). Under such a term a slow sidestep along a gap wall costs nothing and a fast approach costs a lot, the inverse of position-based clearance costs and contact termination. The catch is geometry. CBF-RL and SHIELD model the G1 as a disc, and SHIELD's 0.38 m margin from the center of mass alone implies a clear width of at least 0.76 m ([Yang et al. 2025, SHIELD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11494)). A gap barrier has to act per body point with centimetre margins, like the A1 controller that used a 3 cm minimum distance plus a 6 cm offset to keep it from stopping early ([Liao et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.14199)). This stack's 13 signed-distance samples and its hand and elbow rate inputs already supply what such a term needs.

Model-based legged systems pass narrow gaps by slowing, rotating and then resuming speed, and shape-aware geometry is what makes that work. Liao et al.'s A1 "can slow down and turn its heading 90 degrees to fit itself into the corridor. After the robot enters the corridor, it speeds up to the desired speed", with no hardware collisions, while the same controller with spheres in place of polytopes "gets stuck to entering the corridor" in all 16 trials ([Liao et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.14199)). One-step barrier filters deadlock where an MPC horizon does not ([Thirugnanam, Zeng & Sreenath 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.12313)). Huber et al. remove the doorway stall by capping the summed obstacle influence and amplifying tangential velocity, passing a doorway "marginally wider than the (simulated) robot's diameter" with a proof that trajectories do not stop outside a set gap distance ([Huber et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.04928)). On a G1, ATT makes the turn an explicit command: an effective passage width sets a sidestep ratio between body depth and shoulder width, the side is chosen by minimum yaw and latched with hysteresis "to avoid left/right chatter", and progress speed is "further scaled down" in narrow sections. Its authors trained a sidestep-enhanced AMP policy at 0.2–0.8 m/s because generic priors "degrade when IVH asks for sustained lateral speed", though they report no quantitative results yet ([Ge 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27688)). The classical counterparts are VFH's explicit "narrow valley" selection with a centred path at reduced speed ([Borenstein & Koren 1991](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/integrated1/borenstein_VFHisto.pdf)), guidance computed over (x, y, yaw) so the field itself says "turn, then go" ([Sethian](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/integrated2/sethian_fast_march_nav.pdf)), and min + ε·max arbitration over control points in place of a plain sum ([Barraquand et al. 1992](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/b/barraquand_langlois_latombe_potential.pdf)).

Shields and hierarchies trade rushing for stalling. ABS cut collisions from 15.5–26.1% to 2.9–5.7% by switching to a twist-tracking recovery policy whenever its learned reach-avoid value flagged danger, and the robot "slows down in the tight spaces", but timeouts rose from about 1% to 11.3–16.7%, and "when the obstacles are too dense and form a local minimum, our policy can easily fail" ([He et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.17583)). Time budgets are the other published speed knob: given more time to reach a target, Rudin et al.'s policy "will adopt a slow and safe gait" ([Rudin et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.12827)). Imitation systems get human-paced sidesteps by construction, and MTC adds frames by time dilation wherever the robot cannot match human speed ([Wang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21107)). ABS also supplies a precedent for the real-robot hesitation: without randomizing distant ray readings, "the robot will sometimes tremble near a wall which it has never seen in simulation", and its reach-avoid value, trained on raw binary failure labels, "has local minima in front of the obstacles" until the failure signal is softened ([He et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.17583)).

## Five cheap tests, then remove the per-second hazards

Four hypotheses survive, namely a missing slow lateral gait, a learned barrier at the mouth that only momentum crosses, exposure pricing inside the gap, and missing look-ahead, and cheap simulation tests separate the first three before any retraining. If the policy cannot sidestep at 0.1–0.15 m/s in open space with its body turned 90° to the direction of travel, the gait is missing. If, started at rest and already sideways inside the gap through the existing rest-start mechanism, it walks out at the commanded speed, the barrier sits at the mouth and the burst is how it crosses; if it accelerates anyway, exposure pricing dominates. Querying the critic at the mouth across forced initial speeds shows whether value rises with speed there, the learned counterpart of ABS's velocity-dependent reach-avoid landscape. An offline sweep of `safe_velocity_target` and `_operator_command` over position and yaw for the G1 sites at 0.44–0.48 m widths maps the stop region the reward encodes. Logging in-gap terminations by cause, within half a second of a push or not, against speed sizes the push hazard. The changes below follow in order of expected effect per unit of effort.

Changes 1 and 2 belong together, because they remove the two time-based costs of the same event, and together they reopen the refusal risk: a policy no longer paid to hurry can stall again, so the stall gate relative to the command must stay. Score each run the way the literature does, reporting in-gap mean and 95th-percentile speed against the command at 0.1, 0.15, 0.3 and 0.525 m/s alongside minimum hand clearance, contacts and timeouts, since ABS shows that any safety gain can be bought with stalls.

## Conclusion

The question "does repulsion outweigh attraction at slow speed" presumes an additive controller in which the operator speed scales the pull. This stack normalizes the pull, so any such imbalance can only live in the learned value function, where it is set by what training charges per unit of time near walls rather than by how many points the robot senses. Read that way, the simulated burst and the real-robot hesitation are one phenomenon: the policy values states at the gap mouth more when they carry speed, because slow states there face more expected termination ahead, so a robot that arrives slowly sits in a low-value state and backs off, while one that arrives fast commits.

The durable fix is the property every engineered system shares, that being near a wall costs nothing unless the robot is closing on it, which turns slowness from a liability into the thing that licenses small clearance. For a paper about hand protection the stakes run both ways. Koren and Borenstein's stability bound and the speed-and-separation rule both place the risk at the burst, where latency times speed eats into a sideways G1's clearance, and at 0.9 m/s the robot moves 9 cm between the 10 Hz field updates. A policy that holds 0.15 m/s through a 0.44 m gap is therefore not only more controllable; it is the safety result.
